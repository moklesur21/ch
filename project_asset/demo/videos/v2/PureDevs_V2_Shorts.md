## SHORT 1 — Your customers have another name for it

**Idea:** the gap between your vocabulary and your customers'.
**Proof type:** demo clip (no stat card).
**Runtime:** ~45s · **Plugin named:** no

### On-screen text (0–3s)
> **Your customers have another name for it**
*(7 words)*

### Spoken

Your customers don't use your product names. **[PAUSE]** They use their own words — whatever they'd say out loud to a friend.

And when that word doesn't match anything in your catalogue, the search comes back empty, they leave, and none of it shows up in your orders. The order report only knows about things that sold.

This is a store's top search terms.

Read the proportion, not the number — look at how far ahead the leading term is compared to the rest.

Now look at this one. It doesn't appear in a single product title on the site. **[PAUSE]**

That's not a search problem. That's a naming problem. And you can only see it if something wrote the words down.

### [SCREEN CLIP]
**Screen:** Customer History → Dashboard → Top Searches widget, then click into one term.
**What the viewer should notice:** a ranked list of real words, with a clear leader — then the highlighted term that isn't a product name anywhere in the catalogue.
**Hold:** minimum 5s on the list before any cut. The narration never reads it, so the viewer needs time to read the ranking themselves.

### Caption
Your customers don't use your product names. They use their own — and if that word isn't in your catalogue, the search returns nothing and you never hear about it. Your order report only records what sold. The words people typed on the way in are a different dataset entirely.

## SHORT 2 — Your best-converting surface keeps no record

**Idea:** the search box is the highest-intent surface in the store and the least measured.
**Proof type:** one stat card (no demo clip — the rule is one or the other, never both).
**Runtime:** ~45s · **Plugin named:** no

### On-screen text (0–3s)
> **Your best-converting surface keeps no record**
*(6 words)*

### Spoken

When a shopper searches your store and finds what they were looking for, ninety-two percent of them buy it. Seventy-eight percent buy something else on the way out.

That's Google Cloud and the Harris Poll. **[PAUSE]**

Think about what a search actually is. Someone has told you exactly what they came for, in their own words, unprompted. There is nothing else in your store that does that.

And in a standard WooCommerce install, it's the one surface that keeps no record of what was typed into it.

Your order report is a complete record of what sold. **[PAUSE]** It's a complete record of nothing else.

### [GRAPHIC: stat card — Google Cloud / The Harris Poll, 2023]
> *On screen (9 words):* **Successful search: 92% buy it. 78% buy more.**

Card holds through the first two spoken lines, then clears. No demo footage in this short.

### Caption
A successful on-site search is the highest-intent event in a store — 92% of those shoppers buy the thing they searched for, and 78% add something else (Google Cloud / The Harris Poll, 2023). It's also the one surface a default WooCommerce install keeps no record of. Orders tell you what closed. They can't tell you what was asked for.

## SHORT 3 — Most-viewed and best-selling are different lists

**Idea:** two lists that should match, don't — and the gap is a question worth asking.
**Proof type:** demo clip (no stat card).
**Runtime:** ~55s · **Plugin named:** YES — this is the batch's single plugin-named short.

### On-screen text (0–3s)
> **Most-viewed ≠ best-selling**
*(3 words)*

### Spoken

Quick question you probably can't answer right now: what's the most-viewed product in your store? **[PAUSE]**

Not the best seller. The most looked-at.

Those are two different lists. They're never quite the same list.

Here they are side by side. Read the proportion, not the number.

Watch this one — near the top of the first list, nowhere near the top of the second. A lot of people looked. Very few bought.

Now be careful, because this is where people overreach. That gap is a question, not a verdict. Could be the price. Could be the photos. Could be people researching something they'll buy next month.

But it's the first specific question you've ever had about that product.

That's PureDevs Customer History — and the search and view side of it is free.

### [SCREEN CLIP] — ⚠️ vertical treatment required
**Screen:** Most-Viewed Products and Top-Selling Products.
**Problem:** the long-form records this as a horizontal side-by-side. That crop is unusable at 9:16.
**Solution — capture on the same recording day:** record the two lists as **two separate full-width clips**, then stack them vertically in the edit (most-viewed on top, best-selling underneath) with the mismatched product highlighted in both. A vertical stack reads better than a squeezed side-by-side anyway — the eye travels down the two rankings and finds the break itself.
**Hold:** minimum 5s per list.

### Caption
What's the most-viewed product in your store? Not the best seller — the most looked-at. They're two different lists, and the gap between them is worth a question. Heavy views with light sales might be price, might be photography, might be people researching something they'll buy later. It isn't lost revenue and I wouldn't call it that. It's just the first specific question you've had about that product. Store-wide search and view analytics are in the free tier: https://wordpress.org/plugins/puredevs-customer-history-for-woocommerce/

## SHORT 4 — Some stores can skip this entirely *(proposed alternate)*

**Idea:** small-catalogue stores genuinely don't need this. Saying so is what makes the rest credible.
**Proof type:** one stat card (no demo clip).
**Runtime:** ~50s · **Plugin named:** no
**Status:** proposed, not seeded. Founder's call — see the note below.

### On-screen text (0–3s)
> **Some stores can skip this entirely**
*(5 words)*

### Spoken

If you sell about twenty products, you can probably ignore most of what I've said about on-site search.

Baymard tested niche direct-to-consumer stores and found that only fifteen percent of users ever went looking for a search box at all — and the ones who did only reached for it after browsing had already failed them. **[PAUSE]**

Their own conclusion was that small-catalogue retailers can reasonably put on-site search near the bottom of the list.

On a small, well-organised store, people browse. They don't search.

Search data starts earning its keep when the catalogue is big enough that nobody can see all of it at once. If yours isn't there yet, put the time into your category pages instead.

I'd rather tell you that than sell you a report you won't open.

### [GRAPHIC: stat card — Baymard Institute, 2021]
> *On screen (10 words):* **Niche DTC sites: only 15% of users looked for search**

### Caption
Not every store needs search analytics. Baymard's testing of niche DTC sites found only 15% of users ever looked for a search box, and only after browsing had failed them — their conclusion was that small-catalogue retailers can safely deprioritise on-site search (Baymard Institute, 2021). If you sell twenty products and your categories are clean, people browse. Search data starts mattering when the catalogue outgrows what one person can hold in their head.

### Why this is proposed rather than seeded

It's the highest-trust piece in the batch and the only one a competitor can't copy without meaning it. It's also the one that disqualifies part of the audience in 50 seconds with no recovery.

Two constraints if you run it:
- **Ship it last**, after the parent long-form and the other three shorts are live. It reads as confidence in context and as self-sabotage in isolation.
- The stat is **2021**. It's durable research and it's dated on the card, but it's the oldest evidence in the batch. If that bothers you, drop the short rather than update the date.

Cut it without hesitation if you'd rather run three. It is not padding, but it is optional.

## SANITY CHECK — batch level

**Verified 25 July 2026** (same-session verification as the Final Content Doc and long-form script).

- **Stats used:** DP-1 (Google Cloud / The Harris Poll, 2023) in S2 — verified against the primary Google Cloud blog. DP-6 (Baymard Institute, 2021) in S4 — verified; the corrected 15% figure, not Research B's distorted "100%." No other external figures appear in this batch.
- **No demo-store figures are recited anywhere.** Every demo clip is narrated as proportion, rank or gap: "how far ahead the leading term is," "near the top of the first, nowhere near the top of the second."
- **Competitive claims:** none. No short says only, first, or nobody else. S3 names the plugin but claims nothing exclusive about it — search-term reporting exists in several WooCommerce plugins, at least one of them free (Final Content Doc §9.1), and no short implies otherwise.
- **"WooCommerce has no analytics" never appears.** S2 says a standard install keeps no record *of what was typed into the search box* — narrowly true, verified against core 10.9.4 and the 11.0 pre-release notes, neither of which ships on-site search-term reporting.
- **Observe/report only:** nothing in this batch sends, recovers, automates, discounts or changes store data. S3's landing is explicitly "a question, not a verdict."
- **Accuracy boundary survives compression:** S3 carries it in both the spoken lines and the caption. It's the one thing that must never be trimmed for time — a 55s short that says "views without sales = lost revenue" would contradict the parent video.
- **No prices, no filter counts, no RFM segment counts** — none needed in this topic.
- **Timing note:** if publishing after **28 July 2026**, WooCommerce 11.0 is GA. Its reporting changes (Analytics import retry, refund values in v3 sales-report buckets, stable visitor IDs) do not touch search-term reporting, so S2's claim holds. No re-record needed; re-confirm only if a version number appears on screen.
